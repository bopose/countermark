What AI writing detectors can and cannot show

A short, citable summary of the published evidence — written to be printed and handed to a teacher, marker, or academic-conduct panel alongside your own account of how you worked.

The short version

A detector score is not evidence of misconduct. The peer-reviewed research, and the detector vendors' own statements, show four things:

  1. These tools produce false positives at rates that matter, and they do so unevenly — writing by non-native English speakers is misclassified far more often than writing by native speakers.
  2. Writing by autistic authors is also flagged significantly more often than comparable writing by others.
  3. The company that makes ChatGPT withdrew its own detector because it was not accurate enough.
  4. The mechanism these tools rely on penalises plain, regular, predictable prose — which describes a great deal of honest writing.

None of this proves any particular piece of work is genuine. It does mean a detector score alone cannot establish that a piece of work is not. And if you are disabled, the question may not only be evidential but legal.

If you did use a tool for assistance and are worried about a provenance mark rather than a detector guess, see the separate section on that — it needs a different argument, and the vendors' own documentation helps you make it.

The evidence

1. Detectors misclassify non-native English writing at very high rates

A peer-reviewed study in Patterns (Cell Press) evaluated seven widely-used GPT detectors against 91 human-written TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers and 88 essays by US eighth-grade students. The detectors were near-perfect on the US eighth-grade essays. On the TOEFL essays written by non-native speakers:

  • the average false-positive rate was 61.22% — more than half of genuine human essays flagged as AI-generated;
  • 97.80% (89 of 91) were flagged by at least one detector;
  • 19.78% (18 of 91) were flagged unanimously by all seven detectors.

The authors' own recommendation is unambiguous: they “strongly caution against the use of GPT detectors in evaluative or educational settings, particularly when assessing the work of non-native English speakers.”

2. Autistic writing is flagged significantly more often

A 2026 study tested the claim empirically, running roughly 60,000 Reddit posts through OpenAI's GPT-2 detector, split into a “likely-autistic” corpus drawn from 13 autism-focused communities and a general comparison corpus. Posts from the likely-autistic corpus had a 25% greater chance of being classified as AI-generated (odds ratio 1.25, p = 0.001). When the texts were normalised to the same length to rule out length effects, the gap widened to a 50% greater chance (odds ratio 1.50, p < 0.001).

Read that figure carefully, because the honest version is more useful to you than the dramatic one. The underlying rates were low in both groups: in the length-matched experiment, 1.7% of likely-autistic posts were flagged versus 1.2% of the others. This is not "autistic writing is usually flagged". It is: whatever the baseline rate is, autistic authors are carrying meaningfully more of it than others — a systematic tilt in who absorbs the errors. The authors conclude their findings “add further motivation to examine biases in other AI-detection models and limit or discontinue their use given the potential for harm.”

Notably, the usual explanation does not apply here. The researchers measured perplexity and burstiness — the statistical features detectors lean on — and found them roughly equal or slightly higher in the autistic corpus, not lower. So the tilt is real but its cause is not understood: the tools are penalising something the people who build them cannot account for.

3. OpenAI withdrew its own detector as insufficiently accurate

OpenAI released an AI Text Classifier in January 2023 and discontinued it on 20 July 2023, stating it was “no longer available due to its low rate of accuracy.” On OpenAI's own published evaluation, the tool correctly identified only 26% of AI-written text while incorrectly labelling 9% of human-written text as AI-generated.

4. Even low error rates produce many wrong accusations at scale

Bloomberg Businessweek tested two leading services, GPTZero and Copyleaks, against 500 college-application essays submitted to Texas A&M University in summer 2022 — before ChatGPT's public release, so they could not have been AI-written. The services falsely flagged 1% to 2% of these essays as likely AI-written, “in some cases claiming to have near 100% certainty.”

Turnitin has stated a 4% false-positive rate at the sentence level. Vanderbilt University disabled Turnitin's AI detection over accuracy concerns, noting that hundreds of student papers would otherwise have been incorrectly flagged in a single academic year. Around two-thirds of instructors report using an AI checker regularly, so even a small percentage becomes a large number of students.

How wrong these tools can be is easiest to see on a text whose authorship is beyond dispute: a peer-reviewed 2026 review records the detector ZeroGPT assessing the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines — ratified decades before large language models existed — as 98.5% AI-generated. Formal, structured, repetitive prose reads to these tools as machine-written, whoever wrote it and whenever they wrote it.

Why this falls unevenly on some writers

Most detectors work from perplexity — roughly, how surprising each word is given the words before it. Text that is regular and predictable scores as "low perplexity", and low perplexity is what these tools read as machine-written.

Writing tends to be more regular and predictable when the writer has a smaller working vocabulary in that language, or writes deliberately plainly, or uses consistent sentence structures. That describes a lot of honest writing. The Patterns study demonstrates the mechanism directly: when the non-native essays were rewritten with richer vocabulary, the false-positive rate fell from 61.22% to 11.77%. Nothing about the authorship changed. Only the wording did.

The 2026 review adds a further, uncomfortable asymmetry: because these tools are easily evaded by paraphrasing or translation, a student who deliberately games them is likely to pass, while a student who simply writes in their own plain voice may not. As its authors put it, the system in this configuration “punishes authenticity and rewards evasion.”

The paradox worth putting in front of a panel. The Patterns authors note that “paradoxically, to evade false detection as AI-generated content, these writers may need to rely on AI tools to refine their vocabulary and linguistic diversity.”

In other words, a student penalised for writing plainly can reduce their risk of being falsely accused of using AI — by using AI. Any policy that produces this result is not measuring what it thinks it is measuring.

If you are disabled, this may be a legal question too

This section concerns UK law. The underlying principle — assess the competence, not the medium — travels; the specific duties do not.

There is a further argument here, and it is stronger than an evidential one because it engages a duty rather than a judgement call. A 2026 open-access article in Disability & Society sets it out, building on the Court of Appeal's decision in University of Bristol v Dr Robert Abrahart [2024]. Two points from that ruling matter:

  • A competence standard is not the same as the method used to assess it. For an assessment method to count as an unchangeable competence standard, there must be an “inextricable link” between the method and what is actually being tested. In most subjects the competence being tested is knowledge and analytical skill — not the ability to produce unmediated prose. On that reading the written essay is merely the method, and methods are subject to the duty to make reasonable adjustments.
  • The duty is anticipatory, and does not wait for paperwork. The court found it could apply even without a formal diagnosis, and even where the student had not followed internal procedures, once the university had sufficient actual or constructive knowledge of their difficulties. Commentators have called this a “big leap”: universities cannot hide behind their own bureaucracy.

From this the article argues that regulated use of a language tool may amount to a reasonable adjustment where a student's barrier lies in written expression rather than in reasoning — and that a blanket prohibition is therefore “legally precarious”. Relevant provisions are sections 20 (reasonable adjustments), 19 (indirect discrimination) and 149 (the public sector equality duty) of the Equality Act 2010, and Article 24 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

The parity argument is the one to lead with, because it needs no technical knowledge to follow. A disabled student who dictates to a human scribe is not accused of outsourcing authorship. Proof-reading, supervisory feedback and dictation software already shape a final text and are not treated as misconduct. What matters is whether the ideas originate with the student, not whether their expression was mediated. The article's term for this is “mediated authorship”, and it points out that we accept it elsewhere without hesitation: Socrates wrote nothing down, and H.L.A. Hart's The Concept of Law was completed posthumously by editors working from unfinished notes. We do not reattribute those works. Demanding unmediated prose from neurodivergent students alone is, on this argument, a double standard.

The article also proposes what a fair institution should ask for instead of detection: a short reflective appendix explaining how a tool was used — explicitly parallel to existing disclosure practice for scribes and dictation software, and aimed at “contextual understanding, not surveillance”. That is precisely the document the provenance record builder produces.

If this describes your situation, the question to put to your institution is not only “is this evidence reliable?” but “what is the competence standard this assessment measures, and is unaided fluent prose genuinely part of it?” Your disability service is usually the right route, and worth involving early.

A detail worth mentioning if anyone suggests declaring AI assistance is itself an admission of wrongdoing: the article's own acknowledgements state that its author used a language model as an accessibility support, “assisting with the organisation and refinement of prose, but not in the generation of intellectual content”. It was peer-reviewed and published in a leading disability studies journal on those terms.

A different signal: provenance marks and the word “processed”

Everything above concerns detectors, which guess after the fact. There is a second, quite different mechanism: some AI providers now embed a deliberate mark in the text their systems produce. This is not guesswork — the producer put it there — so it is more reliable than a detector. What it reports, however, is narrower than people assume, and the vendors say so.

Anthropic's own documentation for Claude states:

“Detecting a Claude mark tells you that the content may have been processed by Claude. It does not, on its own, confirm the full provenance of the content.”

“Claude may not be the original author. People often use Claude to proofread, translate, summarize, or convert files. The output can carry a Claude mark even if the underlying ideas, text, or data originated from another source.”

Read that carefully, because it is a rare thing: the company that makes the mark stating in its own documentation that the mark does not establish authorship, and naming proofreading as the example. If your work is substantially your own and a tool was used to tidy the expression, a detected mark is consistent with exactly what you say happened.

Why this is not a "the tool was wrong" argument

This section differs from everything above it, and the difference is worth being honest about rather than blurring.

A detector false positive is an error — the tool said something untrue. But if you used a language model to restructure your paragraphs and the output carries a mark, the mark is correct. The text really was processed by that system. Nothing has malfunctioned.

So the argument here cannot be "the technology is unreliable", and it should not be attempted — it will fail, and it will damage your credibility on the points where unreliability is the issue. The argument is the one set out in the section above: mediation of expression is not outsourcing of authorship. A scribe leaves fingerprints on a text too. The question a fair process must ask is not “was a tool involved?” but “whose ideas and reasoning are these?”

This distinction matters more, not less, as marking improves. An argument that depends on detectors being bad stops working the moment they get good. An argument about what mediation means does not.

A technical note, offered with appropriate uncertainty: these marks are generally embedded by biasing which word the system picks as it generates, which requires that it have a genuine choice to bias. Where a tool reproduces your sentence unchanged it has no such freedom, so an unaltered passage would be expected to carry little or no signal, with strength rising the more the text was actually changed. That is inference from how published schemes of this kind work, not from any vendor's implementation, which is not public. Do not build a case on it.

This page's own tool cannot check any of this. The inspector shipped alongside this document finds hidden characters — it cannot detect a statistical watermark, and says so. Only the vendor that embedded a mark can confirm it.

The regulation behind marking carves out standard editing

Marking of AI outputs is usually explained by reference to the EU AI Act. That Act is worth reading rather than paraphrasing, because its actual text is more favourable to an assisted writer than the summaries suggest.

Article 50(2) requires providers to ensure that outputs are “marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated.”

Note “or manipulated”. An argument that “a human wrote this, so it is not AI-generated content” does not work — editing by an AI system is deliberately within scope, and anyone arguing otherwise will simply be read the paragraph. But the same paragraph continues:

“This obligation shall not apply to the extent the AI systems perform an assistive function for standard editing or do not substantially alter the input data provided by the deployer or the semantics thereof.”

So the point to make is not that marking human writing is unlawful. It is narrower and much harder to dismiss: the regulation cited as the reason for marking contains an explicit exemption for assistive standard editing, and blanket marking does not reflect it. Correcting spelling, grammar and typography is close to the paradigm case of what that carve-out describes.

Three qualifications, so the argument survives being tested:

  • “To the extent” makes the exemption proportionate rather than a switch: it attaches to the function being performed, not to the product as a whole.
  • It fits some uses far better than others. Fixing typos and grammar sits comfortably inside it. Restructuring fragmented ideas into coherent prose — the use with the strongest equality claim — substantially alters the input data even where it preserves the meaning, and so has the weakest claim to this exemption. The two arguments do not cover the same ground, and it is worth knowing which one you are making.
  • A provider cannot easily tell which mode it is in. Nothing in a request announces “this is standard editing”, and under-marking carries more legal risk than over-marking. Blanket marking is therefore understandable caution rather than bad faith — which is precisely why it is a matter for policy argument rather than accusation.

This concerns EU law and is a reading of the published text, not legal advice. If it is relevant to your case, it is the kind of point a disability officer or students' union caseworker can raise with an institution, and it is better documented than most of what circulates about the AI Act.

What a fair process looks like

This is not an argument that AI use should go undeclared. It is an argument about what counts as evidence. Reasonable asks of an institution:

  • Never treat a detector score as sufficient evidence. It is a signal with a documented, unevenly-distributed error rate, not a finding.
  • Say what was actually alleged, and show the output being relied on, so it can be answered.
  • Treat a flag as the start of a conversation, not a verdict. The 2026 review describes institutions using revision opportunities, guided discussion and portfolio assessment for exactly this — restoring the teacher's judgement as the thing that decides.
  • Accept process evidence — drafts, version history, dictation records, a written account of how the work was made. Ask for it before accusing, ideally as normal practice for everyone.
  • Have a declared-use policy so that ordinary assistive use — grammar and spell checking, dictation, screen readers, translation support — can simply be stated rather than concealed. A short reflective appendix, as already used for scribes and dictation, does this without treating the student as a suspect.
  • Do not treat a provenance mark as proof of authorship. It reports that content passed through a system, which is not the same claim — as the vendors' own documentation states. Ask what was mediated and whose reasoning is on the page.
  • Provide a real appeal, with human review by someone who understands the tool's limits. The same review notes that many institutions rely on detector output for high-stakes decisions without adequate appeals processes or staff training.

Being honest about the limits of this evidence

This page argues a case, so it should also say where the case is weaker — a panel will find these limitations anyway, and the argument is stronger for naming them first.

  • The Patterns authors describe their work as a pilot study with relatively small samples (91 and 88 essays), and note that most detectors they tested used GPT-2 as their underlying model. Detector accuracy may differ from the 2023 figures.
  • The 61.22% figure is specific to TOEFL essays, a genre of short, exam-conditions writing. It should not be read as "61% of all second-language writing is flagged".
  • The autism study has real constraints its authors state plainly: it tested one detector (OpenAI's GPT-2 model, since withdrawn), on casual Reddit posts rather than academic essays, and its “likely-autistic” grouping is inferred from which community someone posted in, not from any diagnosis. They argue this noise means the true difference may be larger than measured, but it could also mean the grouping is capturing something other than autism.
  • Turnitin has published its own analysis claiming no statistically significant bias against English-language learners. A panel may raise it. It is a vendor's assessment of its own product, and it does not address the independent findings above, but you should know it exists rather than be surprised by it.
  • Be precise about what the law has and has not decided. Abrahart established the competence-standard/method distinction and the anticipatory nature of the duty. It did not rule that access to a language model is a reasonable adjustment. That is a scholarly argument built on the ruling — a well-founded one, in a peer-reviewed journal, but an argument. Claiming a court has already decided it would be the fastest way to lose the point. The framing to use is: this is the duty; here is how I say it applies to me.
  • The Disability & Society article is a socio-legal and conceptual analysis rather than new empirical evidence, and it is candid about the risks on its own side: dependency where a tool substitutes for learning instead of scaffolding it, ableist bias documented in model outputs, data-protection duties under UK GDPR, and a two-tier problem if only students who can pay get the better tools. Its case is for regulated, institutionally provided use, not a free-for-all — and it notes research finding current tools may fall short of the accuracy needed for high-stakes writing.
  • None of this shows detectors are always wrong, and none of it shows any particular piece of work is genuine. The claim here is narrower and firmer: a detector score is not by itself a sound basis for a finding of misconduct.
